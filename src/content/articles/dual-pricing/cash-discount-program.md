A cash discount program is the oldest legal way for a business to stop paying card processing fees out of its own margin. The idea is simple: the price on the shelf or menu is the card price, and customers who pay cash get a discount. Done right, it is legal everywhere in the US, including California. Done wrong, which is common, it is a surcharge with a cash-discount label and the compliance problems that come with one. This page explains how it works, what the law says, the receipt mistake to avoid, and what it saves.

## What a cash discount program is

Every posted price is the card price. The card price already includes the cost of accepting cards, usually about 3.5% to 4% more than the business would charge for cash. When a customer pays with cash, the register takes a discount off the posted price. When a customer pays by card, they pay exactly the posted price, and nothing is added.

Example with a 3.99% program:

| | Before the program | With a cash discount |
|---|---|---|
| Posted price | $20.00 | $20.80 (the card price) |
| Card customer pays | $20.00 | $20.80 |
| Cash customer pays | $20.00 | $20.00 (a 3.84% discount off $20.80) |
| Business nets on a card sale, after processing | About $19.42 at a 2.9% effective rate | $20.00 |

Notice the percentages. A card price 3.99% above the cash price means the cash discount is 3.84% off the card price. Signage should state the discount as a percentage of the posted price, because that is the price the customer sees.

## Cash discount vs dual pricing vs surcharging

Three programs pass the cost of acceptance to card payers, often sold under each other's names.

| | Cash discount | Dual pricing | Surcharge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Posted price | One price: the card price | Two prices: cash and card | One price: the cash price |
| At checkout | Discount taken for cash | Customer pays the price for the tender chosen | Fee added for credit cards |
| Debit and prepaid cards | Pay the card price | Pay the card price | Cannot be surcharged |
| Cap | None tied to surcharge rules; set at the cost of acceptance in practice | Same as cash discount | 3% for Visa, and no more than your cost of acceptance |
| Notice to your acquirer | None | None | 30 days in writing |
| California | Expressly allowed by Civil Code 1748.1 | Allowed; functionally a cash discount with both prices shown | Restricted by Civil Code 1748.1 and complicated by SB 478 |
| Receipt | Price paid and tender type | Price paid and tender type | Base price plus a surcharge line |
| What customers feel | A reward for cash, if they notice the sign | A choice | A penalty |

## Is cash discounting legal?

Yes, when the posted price is the full card price and nothing is added at checkout. Four layers of rules apply.

**Federal Truth in Lending Act.** Since 1981, 15 U.S.C. 1666f has said a credit card issuer may not, by contract or otherwise, prohibit a seller from offering a discount to induce a cardholder to pay by cash, check or similar means.

**The Durbin Amendment.** The Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 added 15 U.S.C. 1693o-2(b)(2), which says a payment card network may not inhibit a merchant from offering a discount or in-kind incentive for paying by cash, check, debit card or credit card, as long as the discount does not differ by issuer or card network and is disclosed as federal or state law requires. Networks also may not penalize a merchant for offering a compliant discount.

**California Civil Code 1748.1.** Subsection (a) says a retailer may not impose a surcharge on a cardholder who chooses a credit card instead of cash, check or similar means. The same subsection then says a retailer may offer discounts to induce payment by cash, check or other means not involving a credit card, provided the discount is offered to all prospective buyers. That second sentence is the legal basis for every cash discount program in the state.

**California SB 478.** Effective July 1, 2024, the state's "honest pricing" law (Civil Code 1770(a)(29)) prohibits advertising or displaying a price that leaves out mandatory fees or charges, other than government taxes and shipping. This is why the posted price in a cash discount program must be the full card price. If the shelf says $20.00 and the card customer is charged $20.80, the posted price did not include a charge the customer had to pay. If the shelf says $20.80 and the cash customer pays $20.00, the posted price was the full price and the cash customer simply paid less.

**Card-brand rules.** Visa and Mastercard permit cash discounts and do not apply the surcharge cap, the debit ban or the 30-day notice to them, because nothing is added to the card price. What the brands look at is whether the program is really a discount. A card customer paying more than the posted price is a surcharge to them, whatever the program is called.

## The "non-cash adjustment" problem

Most cash discount programs that go wrong go wrong here, usually through the dealer's configuration rather than the merchant's intent.

A non-compliant program leaves the shelf and menu prices at the old cash price. At the terminal, a line is added for card payments: "Non-cash adjustment 3.99%," "Non-cash fee," "Service fee" or "Card adjustment." The receipt shows a subtotal, the adjustment and a higher total. Cash customers pay the posted price. Card customers pay more than the posted price.

That is a surcharge. Visa has told acquirers since at least 2018 that a program which adds an amount to the posted price for card payments is a surcharge, and payment industry compliance guides have repeated the point since. As a surcharge it is capped at 3% for Visa, cannot be applied to debit or prepaid cards, requires 30 days' notice to the acquirer, and in California it is the conduct Civil Code 1748.1 restricts and SB 478 targets.

How to check your own setup:

1. Look at the shelf, menu or price list. Is the price shown the price a card customer pays? If not, you are surcharging.
2. Look at a card receipt. If there is a line called fee, surcharge, adjustment or non-cash adjustment, you are surcharging.
If your receipts have a fee line, fixing it is usually a configuration change plus new signage. We do that for merchants who did not sign with us, too.

## Signage and receipt requirements

A compliant cash discount program needs four things in place before the first sale:

- **Posted prices are card prices.** Shelf tags, menus, price lists, menu boards and the online menu all show the card price.
- **A sign at the entrance and at the register.** Plain wording works: "Our posted prices are card prices. Pay with cash and receive a 3.84% discount." The discount must be offered to everyone.
- **A customer-facing screen that shows the total before payment.** On terminals with a customer display, the card total and the cash total both appear before the customer pays.
- **Receipts that show the price paid and the tender type.** A card receipt shows the posted total and "Card." A cash receipt shows the posted total, the cash discount and the amount paid, or just the cash total. Never a fee line on a card receipt.

Staff script: "Our prices are card prices. If you pay cash, you get a discount."

## How it works on Clover, Dejavoo and other terminals

Cash discount and dual pricing programs are configured at the processor and merchant-account level, not by the merchant in a settings menu. The processor sets the percentage, and the terminal displays the prices and formats the receipt.

- **Clover.** Enabled by the processor during boarding, sometimes with an approved App Market app handling the display and receipt. Station Duo and Mini show the totals on the customer screen before payment. Flex shows them on the screen the merchant turns to the customer. The walkthrough, including what the customer sees, is in [dual pricing on Clover](/clover/dual-pricing).
- **Dejavoo.** Supported at the processor and terminal level, and standard on the Dejavoo terminals we place. The terminal shows both totals before the customer chooses and prints the price paid.
- **Exatouch, PAX and most terminals made in the last five years.** Supported through the processor. Setup takes about a day, and most of that is signage and menu updates.

## What about debit cards?

In a standard cash discount program, debit cards pay the posted card price, the same as credit. That is permitted because nothing is being added: the debit customer pays the posted price and the cash customer pays less. It is also the main financial difference from surcharging, which card-brand rules prohibit on debit and prepaid cards. Depending on the business, 30% to 60% of card transactions are debit, so a program that cannot cover debit leaves a large share of the cost with the merchant.

Federal law does allow a merchant to extend the discount to debit payments, and California's statute allows discounts for any payment that does not use a credit card. Some merchants choose to. It gives up most of the savings and makes the terminal setup harder, so we keep debit at the card price unless a merchant has a specific reason not to.

## What a cash discount program saves

The saving is your current processing bill, less any monthly program or software fee. The card price covers the cost of acceptance on every card transaction, so you net the cash price on card sales.

Three businesses currently paying a 2.9% effective rate, which is typical for a small business on flat-rate or tiered pricing:

| Monthly card sales | Processing cost now (2.9%) | With a cash discount program | Annual saving |
|---|---|---|---|
| $10,000 | $290 | $0 processing | $3,480 |
| $30,000 | $870 | $0 processing | $10,440 |
| $60,000 | $1,740 | $0 processing | $20,880 |

A worked case: a California restaurant with $50,000 in monthly sales, 85% by card. It currently pays about $1,233 a month on $42,500 in card sales. With a 3.99% program, card customers pay $44,196 at the new posted prices, the program's cost comes out of that difference, and the restaurant nets $42,500 on card sales. Cash customers keep paying the old prices, so the $7,500 in cash sales is unchanged. The restaurant keeps about $14,800 a year it used to send to its processor.

## Who a cash discount fits, and who it does not

**It fits:**

- Restaurants, cafes, bars, salons, auto repair and retail that take most payments in person.
- Businesses already quoting one price per item that do not want to print two prices on every tag.
- Service businesses that issue quotes and invoices and can show a card total and a cash or check total on the same document.

**It does not fit as well:**

- Online-only businesses. There is no cash option, so there is nothing to discount. The posted price is simply the card price.
- Retailers selling at manufacturer-advertised prices, where raising the posted price to the card price puts you above competitors' listed prices.
- Businesses with very low card volume, where the saving does not justify new signage and a staff conversation.
- B2B sellers with mostly ACH and check payments. A cash or bank-transfer discount on invoices works, but the conversation with customers is different. Our [invoice dual pricing](/dual-pricing/invoices) page covers that setup.

## Why we usually install dual pricing instead

Cash discounting and dual pricing are the same economics. The card price funds the cost of acceptance, and the cash price is lower. The difference is what the customer sees.

A cash discount shows one price and a sign about a discount. Customers who miss the sign see a higher price than they remember and, when a cash customer behind them pays less, can feel they paid a hidden fee.

Dual pricing shows both prices on the menu, shelf or register sign and on the customer screen before payment. Nobody has to notice a sign to understand it, because gas stations have shown two prices for decades. Under SB 478 the full card price is visibly posted, and under Civil Code 1748.1 it is a discount for cash with full disclosure. It is clearer for customers and easier to defend, which is why it is what we install for most California merchants. Where printing two prices is impractical, we set up a plain cash discount.
